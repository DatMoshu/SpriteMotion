# Sprite content and animation tooling research

Research date: 2026-10-02. This is a source and architecture review, not a benchmark or installation report. Recommendations below are our assessment; capabilities are attributed to the linked upstream projects. Companion: [GUO integration plan](guo-integration-plan.md).

The most useful near-term additions are a GUO importer for completed SpriteMotion jobs, a pixel-editor round trip, and a motion-retargeting experiment. The projects reviewed provide useful components, but none demonstrated the complete combination we need: UO body matching, equipment holdouts, scoped fits, classic animation encoding, and verified client/server deployment.

## Candidates ranked for this project

MIT. | **First code experiment.** Evaluate mask capture, foot pinning and animation synchronization on a procedural fixture. ## What to adopt first

1. **Pixel editing with preserved metadata.** Export one item as frames plus an immutable identity manifest. Paint it in Pixelorama; reimport into a new revision. Demonstrate that an unchanged export round-trips exactly and that an edited frame changes only its designated block. Keep paint corrections separate from 3D fit corrections, and flag them stale when their source render changes.
2. **Capture utilities on a procedural scene.** Compare HardRockTech's equipment-mask and animation-synchronization approaches with our current Blender output. Measure anchor drift, silhouette stability and failure recovery. A normal-map pass is optional; successful UO masking is the gate.
3. **One retargeted animation.** Use a licensed walk or attack clip, explicit source-to-target bone mapping, foot/grip checks, and preservation of animated bone scale. Render all stored directions and mirrored views. Existing equipment that must align to the original UO body should continue using its original matched motions; new motions need matching body and equipment output.
4. **APES annotation pilot.** Compare its proposed masks with hand-labelled low-resolution frames, including crossed limbs, weapons and occlusion. Measure correction time and mask quality on held-out poses. Only promote it if it saves work without hiding uncertainty.

No exact runtimes or quality gains are claimed before these experiments. Pin candidate versions and record input hashes, outputs, dependencies and actual license files when running them.

## Distinguish the different problems

**3D to sprites** is comparatively direct: sample a known rig through a fixed camera. Generic sprite exporters already automate much of this. **New animation to our rig** requires retargeting, contacts and timing. **Old sprites to 3D** is underdetermined: a hidden limb or unseen surface cannot be recovered uniquely from pixels. Research can suggest a fit, but original-view comparisons and human corrections remain necessary.

For SpriteMotion, the scarce part is reliable compatibility with the old game: exact anchors, action IDs, equipment ordering, body masking, palettes, mirroring and a playable staged import. Replacing the renderer with a generic exporter would leave those problems unsolved.

## Tools to keep optional

[Aseprite](https://github.com/aseprite/aseprite) is another useful external pixel editor, but its [EULA](https://github.com/aseprite/aseprite/blob/main/EULA.txt) is not an MIT-style redistribution license. Support interchange files without bundling it. Similarly, keep video reconstruction, image-to-3D models and commercial motion services optional adapters. Public source code, model weights, training data and generated assets can each have different terms.

For a later native GUO authoring workspace, Pixelorama identifies [godot-dockable-container](https://github.com/gilzoide/godot-dockable-container) and [Keychain](https://github.com/Orama-Interactive/Keychain) as UI dependencies. Evaluate them only if GUO's existing docks and shortcuts prove insufficient.
